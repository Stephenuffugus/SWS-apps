# Diamond Rules: where the Everybody Moves assignments come from

Written 2026-08-28, when Stephen's coach asked for a room that teaches the
girls where to go when the ball is hit. Every assignment in `MV_TABLE` in
apps/diamond-rules/index.html traces to a line in one of the documents below.
Nothing in that table was written from memory.

The method: two researchers built the nine player responsibility matrix
independently, from their own sources, without seeing each other's work. A
third pass cross checked them line by line, and every place they disagreed is
recorded below with the reason one was shipped over the other. Anything
neither could source was cut rather than guessed.

The organising idea, and the one line worth teaching on its own, is from
Baseball Positive: "each player on defense has one of three responsibilities:
handle the BALL, cover a BASE, or BACK-UP a throw." The app paints it. Gold
means she is going to the ball, green means she is covering a base, blue
means she is backing up a throw.

## What researcher B reported as conflicting

WHERE MY SOURCES CONFLICT. Five real conflicts, stated rather than resolved.

1. WHO COVERS FIRST WHEN THE FIRST BASEMAN FIELDS THE BALL. Wikipedia "Covering a base" states the general rule: "on ground balls hit to the first baseman away from first base, the pitcher will cover first base instead." Every softball source I found says the SECOND BASEMAN covers instead. The 10U doc: "If the ball is further away than 3 steps, she needs to let the 2nd baseman play the ball and she needs to cover the bag. As your 2nd baseman learns to cover 1st base, you can start to modify the 3 step rule." The softball 2nd base sheet: "If 1st base has to move well away from 1st, then 2nd base person will generally run and cover 1st." The softball bunt PDF: "The 2nd baseman has the responsibility to cover 1st base and receive the throw." The reason given in fastpitch coaching discussion is distance: on a 60 foot diamond with a 43 foot pitching distance the pitcher's head start on the batter is about 17 feet, versus about 30 feet in baseball, so the pitcher often cannot beat the runner there. Baseball Positive resolves it by sending BOTH: the second baseman covers, the pitcher runs to first anyway and backs up the base, crossing behind the runner. That resolution is exactly what Stephen's coach described, and it is what I used in the matrix. But note it IS a resolution, not a consensus.

2. WHAT THE PITCHER DOES ON A BALL TO THE OUTFIELD. Three sources, three different answers, and they cannot all be true. The RPGSA softball playbook has the pitcher BACK UP: behind the rubber with nobody on, behind third with a runner on first, behind home with runners in scoring position. Baseball Positive has the pitcher as the CUT RELAY TO HOME on every ball to the outfield and says explicitly that the pitcher should NOT back up home on a small diamond, "In most cases, there is not enough room behind home plate for the pitcher to get enough depth to properly back up an overthrow." The Defensive Charts PDF has the pitcher at halfway positions and backing up third or home. I used the RPGSA softball version throughout because it is the softball source and because Baseball Positive's cut relay pitcher is designed for teams whose outfielders cannot reach the infield on one throw. A travel coach may prefer the Baseball Positive version. Flag this one for Stephen's coach to choose.

3. DOES THE RIGHT FIELDER CHARGE THE BALL OR BACK UP FIRST ON INFIELD GROUNDERS. Baseball Positive says the corner outfielder on the ball's side sprints AT the ball and "initially, does not have a Backing-up responsibility," with the pitcher backing up the corner base instead. The 8U doc says flatly "The right fielder also has the responsibility of backing up 1st base on all thrown balls," and the 10U doc says "She can't be late backing up the throws to 1st base, she has to be there!" The bunt PDF puts her 20 to 30 feet behind first. I wrote it as a two stage move, charge first then peel to a spot behind first, which is Baseball Positive's own later wording, "Only after the fielder successfully fields the ball does their responsibility change to backing up a throw." The two stage move is my reading, not a direct quote.

4. WHO COVERS SECOND AND WHO COVERS FIRST ON A HIT WITH RUNNERS IN SCORING POSITION. On a single to center with runners on first and second, the RPGSA playbook has the SHORTSTOP cover second and the SECOND BASEMAN cover first. Defensive Charts Figure 9 has the SECOND BASEMAN cover second and the shortstop go out toward the center fielder. I used RPGSA. Similarly, on a single to left with runners in scoring position, RPGSA sends the right fielder to back up a throw toward FIRST while Defensive Charts Figure 3 sends her to back up SECOND.

5. WHICH MIDDLE INFIELDER IS THE CUTOFF ON A BALL TO RIGHT FIELD. The softball 2nd base sheet says "If the ball is hit out to right field, you are the cutoff person." But RPGSA and the Cuts and Relays PDF both make the SHORTSTOP the cutoff on a ball to right field when there is a runner on first, because the throw is going to third, not second. Both are correct inside their own system: the second baseman is the cutoff when the throw goes to second, the shortstop is the cutoff when it goes to third. This is a real trap for an app that shows one answer per zone. I split it by base state in the matrix.

SIXTH, SMALLER: on a ball to the pitcher, Baseball Positive says the shortstop covers second and the second baseman BACKS UP second. The softball position sheets say only which base each takes by ball direction and are silent on the pitcher case. The source itself flags the exception, "a ball hit to the pitcher where the shortstop, thinking they may end up fielding the ball, is unable to cover second base. In this case, the second baseman then takes over," and adds "There is no rule to determine when the second baseman takes over this responsibility." So this one is genuinely fuzzy in the source.

## The cross check, and every resolution

# PART 1. CROSS CHECK OF THE TWO MATRICES

## 1a. Where they agree (ship these without further argument)

Both researchers, working from partly different documents, produced **identical nine-player assignments** for:

- **Ground ball to the pitcher.** Identical, all nine. Both quote Baseball Positive's named exception verbatim: shortstop covers second, second baseman backs up second.
- **Ground ball to the second baseman.** Identical.
- **Ground ball to the shortstop.** Identical.
- **Bunt or slow roller, bases empty.** Identical, all nine, from two different URLs for the same softball bunt sheet. This independent corroboration matters, because Researcher A flagged its bunt PDF as returning two different documents at the same URL. Researcher B's copy is a stable named file (`Basic_Bunt_Coverage.pdf`) and produces the same nine. The bases-empty bunt is safe.
- **Single to left, runner on first.** Identical, and triple-sourced (RPGSA, Cuts and Relays, Defensive Charts Fig 2).
- **Single to left, runners in scoring position.** Identical.
- **Single to center, bases empty / runner on first / scoring position.** Identical in all three.
- **Single to right, runner on first / scoring position.** Identical, triple-sourced.
- **The cutoff table.** Both derive the same five-case rule independently: empty, the middle infielder on the ball's side cuts to second (SS for left and center, 2B for right); runner on first, **the shortstop is the cut to third on all three fields**; scoring position, the third baseman cuts to home on balls to left, the first baseman on everything else. Researcher B's Cuts and Relays source states the runner-on-first case three times in one sentence. This is the most solid block in either document.
- **The ten-word outfield rule.** Left field backs up third, center backs up second, right backs up first. Both call it the single highest-value line in the whole matrix.

That agreement covers the majority of the field. The disagreements below are the whole remaining risk surface.

## 1c. THE STRUCTURAL RULING (D-STRUCTURE): the infield does not change with the runners

Researcher B's most important claim, and it survives audit:

> "In every source, where the nine players MOVE on an infield grounder does not change with the base state. What changes is where the ball gets thrown."

I tested this against Researcher A's own data, since A split by base state. Every single one of A's infield base-state variations collapses:

- Ball to the SS, empty versus runner on first: **the entire infield is identical.** The only differences are which outfielder charges, and those trace to a Wayland line A itself called incoherent.
- Ball to the 1B, empty versus runner on first: A **swaps the two middle infielders** between states, and A's own disagreement note #3 says this is Wayland contradicting itself ("It assigns BOTH the second baseman and the shortstop to the same bag").
- Ball to the 2B, empty versus runner on first: one player differs (D4).
- Ball to the 3B: the only genuine, non-erroneous base-state dependence in either matrix (D6), and it is single-sourced.

**Ruling: the six infield ground-ball zones ship one picture each, valid for all eight runner combinations.** The base state changes exactly one thing, and it is the thing the app already computes: `forces()` gives the lead force bag, which is where the throw goes and what the caption says. This also keeps the Grounders room and this room saying the same thing about the same picture, which matters more than any single assignment.

## 1d. Ground ball versus fly ball: the mode does not ask, and here is the evidence

The geometry specialist argued no toggle from first principles. Researcher B argued it from data, and the data is stronger, so I checked it. Comparing Researcher A's own fly-ball rows against A's own single rows at the same location and base state:

| Same tap, same runners | Identical assignments |
|---|---|
| Left field, bases loaded | **8 of 9** (right field backs up second versus first) |
| Right field, bases loaded | **8 of 9** (second baseman goes out to the fielder versus covers first) |
| Center field, bases loaded | **6 of 9** |

The consistent difference is one middle infielder going out to direct the throw instead of covering a bag. Both are big, visible moves in both versions, so the "everybody moves" lesson is intact either way, and the single rows are the more heavily corroborated ones. **A tap on the dirt plays as a ground ball. A tap on the grass plays as a ball the outfielder fields. There is no gesture, no toggle, no long press.** The app already has a Fly balls tab for the air-specific rules (tag ups, infield fly), and duplicating it here costs words this screen does not have.
