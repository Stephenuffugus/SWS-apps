These are generated live, note by note. There are no audio files in this app. That is why it is one page, works offline, and never repeats itself.

The tempo range is not arbitrary. Trials of music for insomnia keep landing on the same recipe: slow tempo between sixty and eighty beats a minute, one instrument, soft and smooth, simple structure, thirty to forty-five minutes before bed. The tempo slider only moves inside that window on purpose. Jespersen et al., Cochrane 2015 (10 trials, 557 people): music improved sleep quality by about one standard deviation, moderate-quality evidence. Later reviews converge on the 60 to 80 bpm figure.

Texture is what makes a sound itself instead of another shade of noise: the womb's blood-flow swoosh pumping at the heartbeat's own rate, an engine under the road with cars that occasionally pass, raindrops that actually patter, waves that build and break, the flutter of fan blades. Slow-wave bursts
Short pink-noise bursts at 0.8 per second, in alternating on and off blocks. Built to the stimulus described in the published protocol rather than an approximation of it.
Burst rate0.80 Hz
Block length10 s on / 10 s off
Burst level35%
What this is copying. In lab studies, short pink-noise bursts played during deep sleep increase slow-wave activity, and in some studies improve next-morning word recall. The strongest results come from closed-loop systems that read EEG and fire on the up-phase of each brain wave. Open-loop versions, meaning no EEG and fixed timing, which is all a phone can do, have also raised the share of the night spent in N3 in a small supervised study. Schade et al., Nature and Science of Sleep 2020 (n=8, supervised, in-lab): 50 ms pink bursts, 5 ms ramps, one every 1.25 s. Papalambros et al., Front Hum Neurosci 2017 (n=13) for the closed-loop version.
Small studies, lab conditions, EEG supervision. A phone in a bedroom is not that. Treat this as a faithful copy of a research stimulus, not as a device that will reproduce a research result. Set the burst level low, because the studies used levels near the listener's hearing threshold, not loud ones.

Files, not accounts. There is no honest way for a website with no server to log into your watch account and keep your password safe, so HUSH does not try. Instead you export from your tracker and drop the file here. It is read on your phone, never uploaded, and the file itself is not kept.

Live heart rate

Not connected
Works on Android and desktop Chrome only. Apple does not allow web pages to talk to Bluetooth on iPhone or iPad, and the Apple Watch does not share heart rate with anything outside Apple's own apps. That is Apple's decision, not something an app can work around. Chest straps and most Polar and Garmin devices do work.

When a strap is connected, HUSH records the drop in resting heart rate across the settle phase. It is the one measurement here you can watch happen in real time, and it does not depend on anyone's sleep-staging algorithm.

How much to trust the numbers. Wrist and ring trackers are good at telling sleep from wake but only fair to moderate at naming the stage. One validation study put agreement with clinical sleep scoring in the range of a Cohen's kappa of about 0.2 to 0.5 across six devices, and concluded the better ones are suited to tracking prolonged changes rather than judging a single night. That is exactly why this feature randomises and needs a couple of weeks. One night proves nothing. Twenty randomised nights might. Chinoy et al. and related validation work; see also the 11-device multicentre comparison in JMIR mHealth 2023.
The newest and best-controlled study is not good news for all-night noise. A 2026 lab study put 25 adults through seven nights of sleep monitoring. Continuous pink noise did not protect sleep from traffic noise. It made sleep structure worse, and specifically shortened REM. Foam earplugs did work. The authors single out newborns and toddlers as the group to be most careful with, because REM matters most for a developing brain. Basner et al., SLEEP 2026;49(5):zsag001. University of Pennsylvania.
The broader picture was already uncertain. A systematic review of 38 studies graded the evidence that continuous noise improves sleep as very low quality, which sits oddly next to how widely these machines are used. Riedy, Smith, Rocha & Basner, Sleep Medicine Reviews 2021;55:101385.
If you are an adult, try earplugs first. In that same 2026 study foam earplugs did protect sleep, and continuous pink noise did not. A pair costs almost nothing and needs no app at all. Use this instead when earplugs are not an option: when you need to hear a child, when something in your ear is uncomfortable or unsafe, or when what you want is a wind down rather than a block out. None of that applies to babies, who should never be given earplugs.
What this app does about it. Every program here fades out instead of running until morning. Adaptive masking exists so the noise can stay off until something actually needs covering. The volume cap is on by default. None of that makes noise proven. It just means the app is built around the part of the evidence that is least likely to hurt: use it to get through the settling, then let the room go quiet.
Hearing, separately. All fourteen infant sound machines measured in a 2014 study exceeded the 50 dBA nursery limit at 30 cm on maximum volume, and three went past 85 dBA, an occupational limit for adults over eight hours. Distance and volume are the whole fix. That is what the room meter is for. Hugh et al., Pediatrics 2014;133(4):677 to 681. AAP guidance: roughly 2 m / 7 ft from the head.
Where it does look promising. Intermittent stimulation is a different thing from continuous noise. Short pink bursts timed into deep sleep raised slow-wave activity and memory scores in small studies, and an open-loop version raised the share of the night in N3. That is the one genuinely encouraging strand, and it is what the Deep sleep panel implements. Papalambros et al. 2017; Ngo et al. 2013; Schade et al. 2020.
Where these descriptions came from. The notes on each sound were written from published sources, meaning journal papers, Cochrane reviews, garden and instrument histories, by someone outside the traditions several of these sounds belong to. They aim to describe, not to speak for anyone. If something here is wrong, shallow, or misattributed, that is worth fixing and corrections are welcome.

None of this is medical advice and this is not a medical device. If a child's sleep, hearing or breathing worries you, that's a conversation for your paediatrician.
